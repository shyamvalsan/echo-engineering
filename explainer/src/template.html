The composed echo

You clap, and the hillside whistles back. So far, in simulation.

Picture a field of plain posts, a thousand or so, standing in the open with nothing else around. No speakers, no wires, no power. You stand in front of the field and clap once. What comes back is not a smeared echo of your clap, but a short falling whistle, like a bird.

Nothing in the field makes sound. Every post just bounces back a little of your clap, a little later than the one before. Put the posts in exactly the right places and those tiny echoes add up to a sound you chose in advance: a bird call, a few notes, perhaps one day a spoken word.

This project designs such fields on a computer and tests them in a careful simulation. In that simulation the whistle works: a clap comes back as a recognisable falling whistle. Nothing has been built yet. This page explains the idea one step at a time, lets you hear the results, and says plainly how they were judged. Turn your sound on.

An echo is a recording played back by geometry

Start with one wall. Your clap travels out, hits the wall, and travels back. Sound moves at about 343 metres per second, so the further the wall, the longer the wait.

The rule that matters for everything that follows: the sound has to go there and back, so every millisecond of waiting is 17.15 centimetres of distance. Distance in the field becomes time in the echo. A wall is the simplest possible "recording": a single tick, stored as a place.

Many posts, many echoes

Now swap the wall for a row of thin posts, one behind the other, running away from you. Each post returns its own small tick. When the posts are far apart you hear a rattle of separate ticks. Move them closer and the ticks come so quickly that your ear stops counting and hears a pitch instead.

That is the whole trick in miniature. Spacing sets pitch. Change the spacing gradually along the row and the pitch slides. Group posts into bands and you get separate notes.

What works: the whistle

Put that to use. Stand the posts in rings around you, one ring for every ripple of a whistle, and let the rings creep closer together as they go further out. Each ring returns one cycle of the sound, and because the rings get closer, the cycles get shorter and the pitch falls. It is the chirp you hear when you clap in front of a long flight of stairs or a picket fence, bent into a glide.

Three verdicts, kept apart

Why they can differ. An ear that knows what it is listening for is forgiving: it hears a whistle through hiss, and through a pitch that is a hair off. The strict tests are not forgiving: a faint hiss well below the whistle, or posts a couple of millimetres out of place, pull their scores down even when the whistle sounds the same. And knowing the answer makes anyone hear it more easily. That is exactly why a blind test, where the listener does not know what each echo was meant to be, matters.

The layout is the waveform

Go one step further. Any sound can be drawn as a wiggly line, its waveform: how hard the air is pushed, moment by moment. If we want the echo to be that sound, we need the amount of reflecting material at each distance to follow the same wiggle. More posts where the line is high, fewer where it is low. The field becomes the sound written out along the ground, one millisecond for every 17 centimetres, a little like the groove of a record.

There is a catch, and it shapes the whole project. A waveform swings both ways, above and below the line. A post can only push sound back; there is no such thing as a post that reflects "negatively". Switch to magic posts to see what the impossible version would need: the red stretches are places where we would want negative posts.

The fix is to lift the whole wiggle up until none of it is below zero, by adding a smooth, slowly varying background of extra posts. That background, the bias, is nearly silent in the echo, because a clap has almost no deep, low-pitched sound in it to reflect. But it is not free. Something like a third to a half of all the posts are spent just holding the wiggle up, and the useful part of the echo is that much quieter than it could be. This is also why many small posts beat a few big ones. A smooth density of small posts traces a clean wave. A few large posts spaced evenly make the ticks you heard in the row above, which add buzzy overtones to the note.

Why the clap matters

So far the field has been treated as if you could send it a perfect, instant click. A real clap is not a click. It is a short burst of noise, two to five thousandths of a second long, with most of its energy between about 1,000 and 4,000 Hz. Every post returns a copy of the whole burst, so the echo is the designed sound heard "through" the clap.

With an ideal click the echo is the bird note exactly. With a clap it is breathier, and its colour shifts from one clap to the next. Notes far outside the clap's range, very low ones especially, barely come back at all. People clap differently each time, so every design in this project is scored against all five claps, never one lucky one. The designer also compensates: it can put extra posts where the clap is weak, within limits.

How big, how loud

Because distance is time, the length of the sound sets the depth of the field. There is no way around this with plain posts: 17 centimetres for every millisecond.

A bird note fits in a field about the length of a bus. A recognisable tune needs a field longer than a football pitch, because each note has to last long enough to be heard as a note.

Loudness is the other hard limit. Each post is thin, so it returns only a sliver of the clap, and that sliver spreads out in every direction on the way back. Posts far away are fainter still. The whole field adds up, but the echo will be much quieter than the clap. A clap is about 100 decibels at arm's length. Below about 35 decibels, roughly a quiet library, an echo would be lost in the ordinary background noise of most outdoor places.

What we did, and what happened

Written on 25 November. Sentences with numbers in them are filled in from the run files each time this page is rebuilt.

We built two simulators. The fast one treats every post as returning one clean, delayed copy of the clap, like the figures above, and can try thousands of layouts. The careful one follows the actual sound wave through the field, shadows and post-to-post bounces included, and only it may declare a result. An overnight independent review found a flaw in it that bent the pitch by under 1%; it was fixed before any result was accepted.

A designer program turns each target sound into a layout of posts, and strict pass marks, written down before any design existed, score the echo.

Stage by stage

The first lesson: exact rings beat scattered posts. Posts scattered at random each add a little noise; exact rings make every post pull in the same direction, like the fine lines on a CD that split light into clean colours.

The second lesson: a field of posts behaves like slightly thicker air. Sound slows a little among posts, stretching the echo; a slightly stretched layout cancels that. It also fades with depth, as every row takes a little sound from the rows behind, more so with thick posts or a narrow lane. So: thin posts over a wide fan.

The third lesson: the clap and the posts both favour the middle.

Try it yourself

The project's viewer has a Live mode. Clap near your computer and the selected structure answers through your speaker, using the echo the careful simulator computed for it. Then replay your clap and watch the wave roll out through the posts and come back.

  1. Get the project: git clone https://github.com/shyamvalsan/echo-engineering, then, inside it, start the small local server: uv run python -m echo.lab.
  2. Open http://localhost:8000/viewer/ in your browser, choose Live, press Start listening and allow the microphone. Clap once.

The echo you hear is turned up, otherwise you would hear only your own clap; the viewer always shows by how many decibels, next to the true level. Headphones are best for loud settings: through speakers a big boost can feed back into the microphone (the viewer turns the echo down if it hears that happening). This page cannot listen to you itself; the viewer does that on your own machine, and nothing is recorded until you press start.

Design from your own sound. In Live mode, give the designer a short sound file and it lays out a field of posts for it, optionally checked on the careful simulator, and opens it so you can clap at it. Without the viewer: uv run python -m stages.custom --target your_sound.wav --name "my sound".

What a simulation cannot tell us

And some things were left out on purpose:

  • Hearing right after a clap. A loud sound dulls the ear for a few tens of milliseconds, and the echo starts only a couple of hundredths of a second after the clap. Whether that hides the whistle is not simulated; only people listening on a real site can say.
  • Wind and weather. Wind and layers of warm and cool air bend sound and blur the timing. The simulation checks a still, uniform day, plus a cold and a hot one for some designs.
  • The ground and real posts. The real ground reflects too, and real posts have a top. Both are approximated, not simulated, and the careful simulator is flat.
  • Background noise. Traffic, leaves and birds compete with a faint echo. The 35 decibel line is a rule of thumb, not a measurement of any site.
  • Your ears. Pitch trackers and a speech recogniser stand in for a listener, and they can be fooled in both directions.

Nothing on this page shows that a composed echo works in the open air. It shows where the idea is close, where it is far, and why.
