The composed echo

You clap, and the hillside whistles back.

Picture a field of plain posts, hundreds or thousands of them, standing in the open with nothing else around. No speakers, no wires, no power. You stand in front of the field and clap once. What comes back is not a smeared echo of your clap, but a short falling whistle, like a bird.

Nothing in the field makes sound. Every post just bounces back a little of your clap, a little later than the one before. Put the posts in exactly the right places and those tiny echoes add up to a sound you chose in advance: a bird call, a few notes, perhaps one day a spoken word.

This project designs such fields on a computer and tests them in a careful simulation. Nothing has been built yet. This page explains the idea one step at a time, and every figure lets you listen. Turn your sound on.

An echo is a recording played back by geometry

Start with one wall. Your clap travels out, hits the wall, and travels back. Sound moves at about 343 metres per second, so the further the wall, the longer the wait.

The rule that matters for everything that follows: the sound has to go there and back, so every millisecond of waiting is 17.15 centimetres of distance. Distance in the field becomes time in the echo. A wall is the simplest possible "recording": a single tick, stored as a place.

Many posts, many echoes

Now swap the wall for a row of thin posts, one behind the other, running away from you. Each post returns its own small tick. When the posts are far apart you hear a rattle of separate ticks. Move them closer and the ticks come so quickly that your ear stops counting and hears a pitch instead.

That is the whole trick in miniature. Spacing sets pitch. Change the spacing gradually along the row and the pitch slides. Group posts into bands and you get separate notes.

The layout is the waveform

Go one step further. Any sound can be drawn as a wiggly line, its waveform: how hard the air is pushed, moment by moment. If we want the echo to be that sound, we need the amount of reflecting material at each distance to follow the same wiggle. More posts where the line is high, fewer where it is low. The field becomes the sound written out along the ground, one millisecond for every 17 centimetres, a little like the groove of a record.

There is a catch, and it shapes the whole project. A waveform swings both ways, above and below the line. A post can only push sound back; there is no such thing as a post that reflects "negatively". Switch to magic posts to see what the impossible version would need: the red stretches are places where we would want negative posts.

The fix is to lift the whole wiggle up until none of it is below zero, by adding a smooth, slowly varying background of extra posts. That background, the bias, is nearly silent in the echo, because a clap has almost no deep, low-pitched sound in it to reflect. But it is not free. Something like a third to a half of all the posts are spent just holding the wiggle up, and the useful part of the echo is that much quieter than it could be. This is also why many small posts beat a few big ones. A smooth density of small posts traces a clean wave. A few large posts spaced evenly make the ticks you heard in the row above, which add buzzy overtones to the note.

Why the clap matters

So far the field has been treated as if you could send it a perfect, instant click. A real clap is not a click. It is a short burst of noise, two to five thousandths of a second long, with most of its energy between about 1,000 and 4,000 Hz. Every post returns a copy of the whole burst, so the echo is the designed sound heard "through" the clap.

With an ideal click the echo is the bird note exactly. With a clap it is breathier, and its colour shifts from one clap to the next. Notes far outside the clap's range, very low ones especially, barely come back at all. People clap differently each time, so every design in this project is scored against all five claps, never one lucky one. The designer also compensates: it can put extra posts where the clap is weak, within limits.

How big, how loud

Because distance is time, the length of the sound sets the depth of the field. There is no way around this with plain posts: 17 centimetres for every millisecond.

A bird note fits in a field about the length of a bus. A recognisable tune needs a field longer than a football pitch, because notes much shorter than a tenth of a second stop sounding like a melody. The simulation also tries 60 ms notes to see whether a shorter, faster tune still comes through.

Loudness is the other hard limit. Each post is thin, so it returns only a sliver of the clap, and that sliver spreads out in every direction on the way back. Posts far away are fainter still. The whole field adds up, but the echo will be much quieter than the clap. A clap is about 100 decibels at arm's length. Below about 35 decibels, roughly a quiet library, an echo would be lost in the ordinary background noise of most outdoor places.

What we did, and what happened

An interim account, written on the morning of 25 November while simulations are still running. Sentences with numbers in them are filled in from the run files each time this page is rebuilt.

We built two simulators. The fast one treats every post as returning one clean, delayed copy of the clap, just like the figures above; it is quick enough to try thousands of layouts. The slow, careful one follows the actual sound wave as it moves through the field, including posts standing in each other's shadow and sound bouncing from post to post before it comes back. Only the careful one is allowed to declare a pass. Before it was trusted with anything, it was checked against textbook cases whose exact answers are known, such as a single post in open air.

A designer program turns a target sound into a layout of posts. Fixed pass marks, written down before any design existed and never adjusted, then score the echo. For the bird whistle there are two: the pitch must stay within 3% of the target all along the note, and the spectrogram (a picture of which pitches sound when) must match the target's with a score of 0.8 or better, where 1 is a perfect match.

Overnight, an independent review of the careful simulator found a real flaw. Right next to each post it used a cruder calculation, which in dense fields bent the pitch slightly, by under 1%. Small, but about as large as the margin the whistle has to spare, so it was fixed and re-checked before any result was accepted. One limitation remains, stated plainly: for the thinnest posts, the careful simulator is only confirmed for sound bounced straight back, not for sound scattered sideways or forwards, and its handling of dense fields has been checked for consistency with itself rather than against an exact answer.

The redesign under way puts the posts in exact rings instead: one ring for each ripple of the sound wave, so that every post pulls in the same direction. It is the same idea as a diffraction grating, the evenly ruled lines that split light into colours on the back of a CD. The figure below shows the difference.

The night itself went less smoothly than planned: slow downloads held up the start, and an automated worker hung for several hours. So this is an interim account, and the page will be rebuilt as results arrive.

What a simulation cannot tell us

A simulation is only as honest as what it leaves out. These are left out, on purpose, for now:

  • Wind and weather. Wind and layers of warm and cool air bend sound and blur the timing. The simulation checks a still, uniform day, plus a cold and a hot one.
  • The ground and real posts. The real ground reflects too, and real posts have a top. Both are approximated, not simulated.
  • Background noise. Traffic, leaves and birds will compete with a faint echo. The 35 decibel line above is a rule of thumb, not a measurement of any site.
  • Your ears. Pitch trackers and a speech recogniser stand in for a listener, and they can be fooled in both directions. The real test is a person listening to the audio files, which is why every result above has a play button.

The open questions are concrete: is the echo loud enough to hear outdoors, how short can the notes get before a tune stops being a tune, and can plain posts ever carry speech, whose low, rich sounds are exactly what thin posts and claps are worst at.

How it could be built

The plan for a real structure goes in three steps, each cheap enough to fail at:

  1. A tabletop model at one tenth scale. Shrink the field ten times and raise every pitch ten times, into ultrasound. The physics is the same, so a field that fits on a table can test the designs cheaply.
  2. A full-size outdoor trial. A real bird-note field, the smallest design, in a real open space, with real claps and real wind.
  3. A permanent structure. Only if the first two work, and only at a size the site can hold.

The composed echo is a design study: a simulator and an inverse designer for passive acoustic structures.
